"Will it hurt?"

It is the first thing most patients ask, often before they have even sat down. And it deserves a real answer rather than a reassuring "no, not at all" — because if a patient expects zero sensation and then feels pressure, they conclude something has gone wrong.

So here is the honest version, treatment by treatment.

## What each treatment actually feels like

![Comfort expectations for scaling, fillings, root canal, extraction and implant placement](/images/blog/painless-visit.svg)

## The distinction that matters: pressure vs pain

Local anaesthetic blocks **pain**. It does not block **pressure**, **vibration**, or **sound**.

This is the single most useful thing to understand before any dental procedure. During an extraction you will feel firm pushing. During an implant placement you will feel vibration. Neither is pain, and neither means the anaesthetic has failed.

Once patients know to expect pressure, the experience changes completely — because the sensation is no longer evidence that something is wrong.

## Why a toothache hurts more than the treatment for it

This surprises people, but it is consistently true: the root canal is almost always less painful than the days leading up to it.

An inflamed or infected nerve inside a tooth generates constant, throbbing pain that painkillers barely touch. Root canal treatment removes that nerve. The procedure is done under anaesthetic; the pain that brought you in is not.

If you are avoiding treatment because you are afraid it will hurt, you are very likely choosing the more painful path.

## What we do to keep it comfortable

- **We wait for the anaesthetic to work properly.** Rushing this is the most common cause of an uncomfortable appointment. We test the area before starting.
- **We tell you before we do anything.** No surprises. If you know pressure is coming, it does not startle you.
- **A raised hand stops everything.** Immediately, no questions. Knowing you have that control makes a genuine difference to how the appointment feels.
- **We use modern rotary instruments.** These are faster and gentler than older techniques, which means less time in the chair.

## If you are genuinely anxious, please say so

Dental anxiety is extremely common and nothing to apologise for. Some of our patients have avoided dentists for years before walking in.

Tell us at booking, not in the chair. It changes how we plan the appointment: we allow more time, we can do a first visit that is nothing but a conversation and a look, and we break treatment into shorter sessions if that helps.

Several of our patients arrived describing themselves as "scared of dentists." What changed for them was not willpower — it was being given time, information, and control.

## Managing the afterwards

For most treatments, this is simpler than people expect:

- **Fillings** — eat normally once numbness fades (1–2 hours). Avoid biting your numb cheek in the meantime.
- **Root canal** — mild tenderness for 2–3 days. Ordinary painkillers are enough. Avoid heavy chewing on that tooth until the crown is fitted.
- **Extractions** — soft food, no straws, no smoking for 48 hours. Straws and smoking create suction that can dislodge the healing clot, which genuinely does hurt.
- **Implants** — mild soreness for up to a week. Most people are back at work the next day.

Call us if pain gets worse rather than better after day three, or if swelling increases. That is not normal, and it is easily addressed early.

## The takeaway

Modern dentistry is not the experience your parents describe. The techniques, the anaesthetics, and the instruments have all changed.

What has not changed is that avoiding a small problem reliably turns it into a bigger, more uncomfortable, more expensive one. A cavity treated now is a twenty-minute filling. The same cavity in two years may be a root canal.
