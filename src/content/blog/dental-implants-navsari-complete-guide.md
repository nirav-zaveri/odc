Losing a tooth is not just a cosmetic problem. It changes how you chew, how you speak, and — over time — the shape of your jaw. Of all the ways to replace a missing tooth, a dental implant is the closest modern dentistry gets to giving you your own tooth back.

Implants are also the treatment our patients ask the most questions about, and worry the most about. So here is the honest version.

## What a dental implant actually is

An implant is not a whole tooth. It is three separate parts:

1. **The implant** — a small titanium post that takes the place of your natural tooth root, placed into the jawbone.
2. **The abutment** — a connector that sits on top of the implant, at gum level.
3. **The crown** — the visible ceramic tooth, custom-made to match your other teeth.

The reason implants feel so natural is that the titanium post genuinely fuses with your bone — a process called osseointegration. It is not glued or clipped in. It becomes part of your jaw.

## The four stages, start to finish

![The dental implant journey: assessment, placement, integration, and the final crown](/images/blog/implant-journey.svg)

### Stage 1 — Digital assessment

Before anything is placed, we take a digital X-ray to see your bone height, bone density, and where your nerves and sinuses sit. This is the stage that determines whether an implant will succeed, so we do not rush it.

### Stage 2 — Placement

The implant is placed under local anaesthetic, usually in a single appointment. You will feel pressure and some vibration — you should not feel pain. This is the step patients dread most and are most surprised by afterwards.

### Stage 3 — Integration

Over roughly three to six months, bone grows around the implant and locks it in place. You carry on with normal life during this period; if the gap is visible, we can fit a temporary tooth.

### Stage 4 — Your final crown

Once the implant is stable, we fit the permanent ceramic crown, shade-matched to your neighbouring teeth. From this point, you brush, floss, and eat exactly as you would with a natural tooth.

## Are you a good candidate?

Most adults are. The things that genuinely matter:

- **Enough healthy bone.** If bone has shrunk after long-term tooth loss, a bone graft may be needed first.
- **Healthy gums.** Active gum disease must be treated before implant placement.
- **Diabetes under control.** Well-managed diabetes is not a barrier; poorly-controlled diabetes slows healing.
- **Not smoking.** Smoking measurably increases implant failure rates. If you smoke, we will talk honestly with you about this.

Age, by itself, is almost never a disqualifier.

## Implant vs bridge vs denture

| | Implant | Bridge | Denture |
|---|---|---|---|
| Affects neighbouring teeth | No | Yes — they must be filed down | No |
| Prevents bone loss | Yes | No | No |
| Feels fixed | Yes | Yes | Removable |
| Typical lifespan | 15+ years, often lifelong | 7–15 years | 5–8 years, needs relining |
| Upfront cost | Highest | Middle | Lowest |

An implant costs more on day one. Over fifteen years, it is frequently the cheaper option — and it is the only one of the three that stops the jawbone under the gap from shrinking.

## The questions we get asked most

**"How long will it last?"**
With good hygiene and regular check-ups, implants routinely last well over fifteen years, and often for life. The crown on top may need replacing sooner than the implant itself.

**"Will people be able to tell?"**
A well-made implant crown is very difficult to distinguish from a natural tooth, because the shade, translucency, and shape are matched to the teeth beside it.

**"Can I have more than one?"**
Yes. Several implants can support a bridge, or an entire arch of teeth, which is often a better solution than a full denture.

**"What if it fails?"**
Implant failure is uncommon but not impossible. If an implant does not integrate, it is removed, the site is allowed to heal, and placement can usually be re-attempted.

## A note on choosing where to have it done

Implant dentistry is unusually dependent on planning. The surgery itself is often the easy part; the difference between an implant that lasts twenty years and one that fails in two is usually the assessment before it, and the follow-up after it.

Ask any clinic you are considering: will you take a digital X-ray before planning? What implant system do you use? What happens if there is a problem in year three? You deserve clear answers to all three.
